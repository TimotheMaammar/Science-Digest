# Concentration of violent crime

## About

Title : The 1 % of the population accountable for 63 % of all violent crime convictions

Link : https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3969807/

Researchers : Örjan Falk, Märta Wallinius, Sebastian Lundström, Thomas Frisell, Henrik Anckarsäter, Nóra Kerekes

Date : 2014

## Synthesis

A nationwide Swedish register study covering every person born in Sweden between 1958 and 1980 (2,393,765 individuals), followed from 1973 to 2004, that quantifies just how concentrated violent crime is in a population. The quality of the data is exceptional: a unique personal ID links the national crime, population, death, school, and hospital registers, and the Swedish legal system records every conviction (plea-bargaining is banned, and even mentally ill offenders are convicted and then referred to forensic care, so no one is filtered out). The headline finding: 93,642 individuals (3.9% of the population) were convicted of at least one violent crime, but within that group, 24,342 persistent offenders, just 1.0% of the total population, accounted for 63.2% of all violent crime convictions and 59.5% of all aggravated violent crimes. The distribution is extraordinarily skewed, with a small core of high-persistence offenders (0.1% of the population) averaging 16.5 violent convictions each.

The study also maps how reoffending escalates. After a first violent conviction, 44% are reconvicted; after the second, 59%; after the third, 68%. The probability of further violence rises with each conviction, which is why the authors argue that stopping violent careers after the third conviction, an intervention targeting 1% of the population, would in principle prevent more than half of all violent convictions. A clear gender gradient runs through everything: women are 10.8% of violent offenders overall but only 2.1% of the high-persistence group, and men commit roughly 90% of violent crime.

A multivariate regression identified the strongest predictors of persistence: male sex (OR 2.5), personality disorder (OR 2.3), a first violent conviction at age 15-18 (OR 2.0), theft (OR 2.0), drug offenses (OR 1.9), and substance use disorder (OR 1.9). A politically important and counterintuitive result is that major mental disorders (schizophrenia, bipolar) account for only 3.7% of violent convictions and are only weakly tied to persistence (OR 1.3); it is personality disorders and substance abuse, not severe mental illness, that drive repeat violence. The main caveats are that the design is cross-sectional (so the exact ordering of risk factors and crime cannot always be established), psychiatric diagnoses come only from inpatient records (underestimating true prevalence), and socioeconomic factors were deliberately excluded, so the distribution findings generalize mainly to Western Europe and Canada while the individual risk factors are likely broader.
