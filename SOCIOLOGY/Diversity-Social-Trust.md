# Ethnic diversity and social trust

## About

Title : Ethnic Diversity and Social Trust: A Narrative and Meta-Analytical Review

Link : https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-polisci-052918-020708

Researchers : Peter Thisted Dinesen, Merlin Schaeffer, Kim Mannemar Sønderskov

Date : 05/2020

## Synthesis

A meta-analysis of 1,001 estimates from 87 studies spanning the US, Britain, Germany, Denmark, Sweden, Norway, Australia and the Netherlands, built to move the field beyond single-country, single-dataset findings and quantify the overall relationship between local ethnic diversity and social trust. The core finding: a statistically significant negative relationship holds across all studies.

Breaking the effect down by trust target adds precision most single studies lack. The negative relationship holds for trust in neighbors, in-group trust and generalized trust, roughly doubling in strength for neighbor trust versus generalized trust, but it is not statistically distinguishable from zero for out-group trust specifically. This pattern runs counter to classic group-threat/conflict theory (which predicts eroded out-group trust and, in its stronger versions, boosted in-group trust) and instead supports an anomie-style mechanism, since in-group trust erodes right alongside the rest rather than rising as compensation. The effect is also strongly local: nearly three times stronger when diversity is measured at the neighborhood level than at the country level, consistent with physical proximity to out-group members being the operative condition, not diversity as an abstract national statistic.

Robustness checks matter here more than in most single studies. Controlling for individual socioeconomic status, contextual deprivation and contextual crime barely moves the estimate; only controlling for individual minority status meaningfully attenuates it (by about a third), and even then the relationship stays significant by a wide margin, indicating the result is a genuine contextual effect of diversity rather than a compositional artifact of minorities (who trust less on average) being overrepresented in diverse areas. The review does flag the field's central weakness directly: the overwhelming majority of underlying studies are cross-sectional and observational, so causal claims aren't warranted, and it discusses reanalyses of individual studies in the underlying dataset that lose significance once additional concentration variables are added, while also noting those added controls are themselves often collinear with ethnic diversity, leaving the true bearing of such reanalyses on the broader pooled result unresolved. A striking macro-level counterpoint closes the review: Denmark diversified sharply after 1980 while generalized trust rose from 47% to 79% between 1979 and 2009, even though local-level diversity in Danish neighborhoods, municipalities and workplaces is itself negatively associated with trust over the same period, a divergence between micro and macro patterns the authors leave as an open question for future research.
