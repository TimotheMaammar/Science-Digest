# IQ and road accident mortality

## About

Title : Intelligence and behaviour and motor vehicle accident mortality

Link : https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/000145759090013B

Researcher : Brian I. O'Toole

Date : 1990

## Synthesis

A striking demonstration that intelligence predicts not just life outcomes but literal survival, specifically death by car crash. O'Toole examined every Australian former National Service conscript of the Vietnam era, comparing all those who had died in motor vehicle accidents (MVAs) between basic training and 1982 against a random sample of survivors, using army records that included a standardized intelligence test taken at enlistment. Because conscription captured a broad cross-section of young men and the cognitive data were collected years before the deaths, the design sidesteps reverse causation: the IQ score came first, the fatal crash came later. The decedents were in their twenties and thirties, making this a study of premature death in young, generally healthy men.

The core finding is that risk of death by motor vehicle accident was higher for men with lower scores on the army intelligence test, alongside poorer education, lower pre-enlistment occupational status, a history of juvenile offenses, and going AWOL during service. When these were modeled together with logistic regression, intelligence combined with either lower education or a prior offense history emerged as the most important predictors of MVA mortality. This matters because road accidents are one of the leading causes of death in working-age men, so a cognitive predictor of fatal crashes is a meaningful piece of the broader picture linking IQ to longevity. It gives a concrete behavioral mechanism: higher cognitive ability plausibly translates into better real-time risk assessment, hazard anticipation, and judgment behind the wheel.

This study sits naturally alongside the larger cognitive-epidemiology literature showing that childhood and youth IQ predicts all-cause and accident mortality decades later. The accident pathway is one of the more intuitive mechanisms in that body of work: unlike disease outcomes, which may involve healthcare navigation or physiological robustness, fatal accidents speak directly to moment-to-moment decision-making and impulse control. The main caveats are that the cohort is exclusively male and military, the intelligence measure is a single army screening test rather than a full IQ battery, and the observational design cannot fully exclude that low IQ co-travels with other risk factors (risk-taking temperament, alcohol use) that do the causal work.
