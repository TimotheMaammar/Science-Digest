# IQ and health outcomes

## About

Title : Intelligence in youth and all-cause mortality: systematic review with meta-analysis

Links : 
- https://academic.oup.com/ije/article-abstract/40/3/626/742085
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21037248/

Researchers : Catherine M. Calvin, Ian J. Deary, Candida Fenton, Beverly A. Roberts, Geoff Der, Nicola Leckenby, G. David Batty

Date : 10/2010

## Synthesis

The most rigorous quantification of the IQ-mortality relationship, covering 16 independent prospective cohort studies with 1,107,022 participants and 22,453 deaths observed over follow-up periods ranging from 17 to 69 years. The core finding: a one standard deviation advantage in youth cognitive test scores (roughly 15 IQ points) is associated with a 24% lower risk of all-cause mortality. This places IQ among the strongest predictors of premature death known, comparable to or stronger than many conventional risk factors including blood pressure, cholesterol levels, and BMI, as shown in a companion paper by Batty et al. (2008).

The association holds across causes of death. Higher IQ in youth predicts lower mortality from cardiovascular disease, cancer, accidents, and suicide, suggesting the mechanism is broad rather than disease-specific. The accident finding is particularly striking: unintentional injuries, one of the leading causes of premature death in working-age populations, show a strong inverse relationship with IQ. This is consistent with the idea that IQ captures something like practical risk assessment and environmental navigation, not just academic ability. For specific physical health outcomes, IQ also predicts lower rates of type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and obesity, and higher lung function, independently of SES in most studies. The leading hypotheses are not mutually exclusive: higher-IQ individuals adopt healthier behaviors, navigate healthcare systems more effectively, select into safer environments and occupations, and may have underlying physiological robustness that simultaneously drives both cognitive ability and longevity. Critically, several studies in the review show the association persists after controlling for childhood socioeconomic status, suggesting IQ is not simply a proxy for parental wealth.

A 2023 update by Sanchez-Izquierdo et al. (Intelligence) confirmed the direction and magnitude of the effect (HR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.76-0.81) while adding an important moderator: the protective effect of intelligence is strongest in middle adulthood and attenuates with age, suggesting that from late old age onward, biological factors become increasingly dominant over cognitive ones.
