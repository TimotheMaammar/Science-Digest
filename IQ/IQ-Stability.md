# Lifelong stability of IQ

## About

Title : "The Stability of Individual Differences in Mental Ability from Childhood to Old Age: Follow-up of the 1932 Scottish Mental Survey"

Links :

- <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0160289699000318>
- <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0160-2896(99)00031-8>

Researchers : Ian J. Deary, Lawrence J. Whalley, Helen Lemmon, J.R. Crawford, John M. Starr (University of Edinburgh, University of Aberdeen)

Date : 2000

## Synthesis

The longest follow-up study of human intelligence ever conducted, spanning almost an entire lifetime. In 1932, the Scottish Mental Survey tested nearly every Scottish child born in 1921 (N = 87,498) on the same validated instrument, the Moray House Test. Decades later, the researchers tracked down 101 of these individuals at age 77 and had them sit the exact same test again. The headline result: the correlation between IQ measured at age 11 and at age 77 was 0.63, rising to 0.73 after correcting for the restricted ability range of the surviving sample. Over a 66-year gap, that is a remarkably high figure, meaning that a large share of the differences in cognitive ability in old age is already present and predictable in childhood.

The crucial distinction this study makes clear is between absolute score and relative standing. The absolute mean score actually rose between the two testing sessions, consistent with practice effects and the broader Flynn Effect. What stayed stable was each person's rank relative to others: the bright 11-year-olds were, by and large, still the bright 77-year-olds. Intelligence as a trait is therefore not frozen in absolute terms, but the ordering of individuals persists across the whole lifespan. This reframes IQ as one of the most temporally stable psychological traits known.

The practical importance is greatest for the study of cognitive decline and dementia. One of the hardest problems in geriatric cognition is that without a baseline from earlier in life, a low score in old age cannot be distinguished from lifelong low ability versus genuine decline. This study shows that a childhood measure remains a valid reference point six decades later, providing the anchor needed to measure true cognitive aging. It also helps explain why IQ predicts later-life outcomes like longevity and health: those associations work precisely because the trait being measured is so durable. The main caveat is survivor bias, since the 101 people retested at 77 were a healthier, selected subset, which is exactly why the range-corrected figure of 0.73 is the more accurate estimate.
