# IQ and socioeconomic success

## About

Title : Intelligence and socioeconomic success: A meta-analytic review of longitudinal research

Link : https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0160289606001127

Researcher : Tarmo Strenze (University of Tartu, Estonia)

Date : 2007

## Synthesis

The most comprehensive longitudinal meta-analysis on intelligence and career success, covering 85 datasets and 135 samples drawn primarily from the US, UK, Scandinavia, and New Zealand. By restricting inclusion to longitudinal studies where IQ was measured before adulthood and success was assessed at least a decade later, the study directly addresses causality rather than mere cross-sectional correlation. The best-study estimates place the corrected correlations at r = 0.56 with educational attainment, r = 0.45 with occupational status, and r = 0.23 with income, all measured after age 29.

The central finding that makes this study worth archiving is the three-way comparison. IQ is not overwhelmingly stronger than parental socioeconomic background or school grades as a predictor of success. The composite parental SES index comes close to matching IQ for education and occupation, and neither parental background nor grades outcompete IQ either. The reasonable conclusion is that intelligence, social origin, and academic performance are roughly co-equal determinants of adult outcomes, each contributing independently. This directly falsifies the claim that the IQ-success correlation is simply a proxy for parental wealth or schooling.

Two moderator findings are worth noting. First, the predictive validity of IQ for occupational status and income grows stronger as individuals age, consistent with the gravitational hypothesis that people drift toward positions matching their ability over time. The correlation between IQ and income is near zero before 25 and climbs to r = 0.27 by ages 30-34. Second, there is no evidence of any historical trend: the importance of IQ for career success has not increased or decreased across the decades covered by the dataset, contrary to claims made in The Bell Curve for example.
