# IQ and fertility: dysgenic trends internationally

## About

Title : International meta-analysis of the relationship between fertility and intelligence

Link : https://openpsych.net/files/papers/Jensen_2025a.pdf

Researchers : Sebastian Jensen, Bryan J. Pesta, Emil O.W. Kirkegaard

Date : 08/2025

## Synthesis

The most comprehensive international quantification of the relationship between intelligence and fertility to date, covering 65 countries with n = 419,444 (k = 156) for direct IQ-fertility data and n = 797,455 (k = 454) for education-fertility data, drawn from PISA, PIRLS, PIAAC, the World Values Survey, the GSS, and both NLSY waves. The central finding: the average country's IQ is declining by approximately 0.35 points per decade due to differential fertility, with a projected global decline of 1.1 points per decade when between-country population differences are factored in. The overall direction is consistent with decades of prior research concentrated in the United States and UK, now extended internationally for the first time with sufficient power.

The negative correlation between IQ and fertility is robust across most regions but varies substantially by geography. The effect is strongest in Latin America, Iran, and Turkey, moderate in Anglo countries and East Asia, and negligible in Scandinavia, where Denmark, Finland, Estonia, and Switzerland show selection differentials close to zero. The one legitimate counterexample with a serious sample is Kolk and Barclay (2019, Proceedings of the Royal Society B), a large Swedish conscription cohort showing a weak positive relationship between cognitive ability and fertility in men. The paper's own analysis reconciles this: the dysgenic pattern is consistently stronger in women than men across all datasets, so male-only samples are expected to understate or reverse the effect. No large study with a representative mixed-sex sample shows a clearly positive relationship.

Two nuances are worth retaining. First, the relationship between IQ and fertility is largely mediated by educational attainment, which correlates with dysgenic fertility at r = .62 across countries. This does not diminish the finding but clarifies the pathway: higher IQ leads to more education, which delays and reduces reproduction, especially in women. Second, the projected decline does not account for the Flynn Effect, the secular phenotypic rise of roughly 3 IQ points per decade observed through much of the 20th century. That rise has stalled or reversed in several Northern European countries since roughly 2000, which the paper interprets as consistent with the dysgenic hypothesis beginning to outpace environmental gains. However, the magnitude and universality of this reversal remain debated.

A methodological note: OpenPsych is a peer-reviewed open-access journal outside the mainstream, with a small editorial board. The data sources used here (PISA, PIAAC, NLSY) are legitimate and large, and 98.5% of the effect sizes are unpublished, meaning publication bias is negligible. The conclusions are directionally consistent with the broader literature. The paper should be read as the best current international synthesis, not as settled consensus.
