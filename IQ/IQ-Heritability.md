# Heritability of intelligence

## About

Title : Meta-analysis of the heritability of human traits based on fifty years of twin studies

Link : https://www.nature.com/articles/ng.3285

Researchers : Tinca J.C. Polderman, Beben Benyamin, Christiaan A. de Leeuw, Patrick F. Sullivan, Arjen van Bochoven, Peter M. Visscher, Danielle Posthuma

Date : 2015

## Synthesis

The largest meta-analysis of twin studies ever conducted, compiling twin correlations and variance components for 17,804 traits from 2,748 publications and 14,558,903 partly dependent twin pairs, covering virtually every twin study published between 1958 and 2012 across 39 countries. The design exploits a simple logic: monozygotic twins share ~100% of their DNA while dizygotic twins share ~50%, so the gap between how similar each type of twin pair is allows the variance in a trait to be split into genetic and environmental components. Across all human traits, the average reported heritability is 49%, and strikingly, not a single trait had a heritability of zero. For 69% of traits, the data fit a simple additive genetic model with no substantial role for shared environment or gene interactions.

For intelligence specifically, the picture is more nuanced and worth archiving carefully. Higher-level cognitive function is one of the minority of traits that does not fit the simple additive model, meaning shared environment (the family home, schooling) retains a measurable role. This is why the often-quoted heritability figure of 0.70 to 0.80 applies only to adults in high-income Western samples, not as a global average. The heritability of IQ is not fixed: it rises from around 40% in childhood through roughly 60% in adolescence to 70-80% in adulthood. This counterintuitive increase suggests genetic influences amplify over development rather than being diluted by accumulating experience.

Two mechanisms are thought to drive this age trajectory, and the study cannot fully separate them. The first is active gene-environment correlation: as individuals gain autonomy, they increasingly select, create, and evoke environments aligned with their genetic predispositions, which amplifies the expression of those predispositions. The second is biological maturation, where genes affecting brain function express their full effect only as neural structures mature. In parallel, the shared environment component, which is substantial in childhood, shrinks toward near zero in adulthood, leaving genetic factors and idiosyncratic non-shared experiences as the dominant sources of variance. Importantly, high heritability does not mean immutability: height is roughly 90% heritable yet rose dramatically across the 20th century through better nutrition, and the Flynn Effect shows the same logic applies to IQ.
