# Genetic proximity of Europeans

## About

Title : Genes mirror geography within Europe

Link : https://www.nature.com/articles/nature07331

Researchers : John Novembre, Toby Johnson, Katarzyna Bryc, Zoltan Kutalik, Adam R. Boyko, Adam Auton, Amit Indap, Karen S. King, Sven Bergmann, Matthew R. Nelson, Matthew Stephens, Carlos D. Bustamante 

Date : 08/2008

## Synthesis

A landmark study on the fine-scale genetic structure of Europe, analyzing 1,387 individuals from more than 30 countries genotyped at over 500,000 variable DNA sites. Using principal component analysis to compress that variation into two dimensions, the authors found something striking: the resulting genetic map reproduces the geographic map of Europe with remarkable fidelity. The Italian boot, the Iberian peninsula, Scandinavia in the correct order, and Cyprus off the Greek coast are all visible. From DNA alone, 90% of individuals can be placed within about 700 km of their actual geographic origin, and the resolution is fine enough to distinguish French, German, and Italian speakers within Switzerland, and to separate Ireland from the UK.

The reason this study is worth archiving is that it carries two messages that seem opposed but are both true. First, Europeans show very low average genetic differentiation: on the scale of worldwide human variation, they form a tight, genetically homogeneous cluster, and the distance between, say, a Portuguese and a Russian individual is minuscule at the species level. Second, despite that closeness, the residual variation is so finely organized by geography that it suffices to rebuild a map of the continent and to locate individuals. Low differentiation does not mean absence of structure. The variation is continuous and clinal rather than discrete, which is directly relevant to the question of human "races": geographic gradients are real and measurable, but they have no sharp boundaries.

This structure arises from isolation by distance, migration, and geographic barriers acting over many generations, with the historical origin of the gradients later clarified by ancient DNA work (the hunter-gatherer, Anatolian farmer, and steppe pastoralist layers). The study also delivered a major methodological lesson for medical genetics: in genome-wide association studies, failing to account for population structure can produce spurious gene-disease associations, since a variant common in one region for unrelated historical reasons can be falsely linked to a disease that happens to be common there too. Correcting for this structure via PCA has since become standard practice.
