# Genetics of crime: Danish twin study

## About

Title : A preliminary study of criminality among twins (in Biosocial Bases of Criminal Behavior)

Link : https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/preliminary-study-criminality-among-twins-biosocial-bases-criminal

Researchers : Karl O. Christiansen (University of Copenhagen)

Date : 1977

## Synthesis

The classic twin study of criminality, drawing on an unselected sample of 3,586 twin pairs born in the eastern half of Denmark between 1881 and 1910, in which both twins survived to at least age 15. The twin method works by comparing concordance rates (how often both twins share a trait) between monozygotic (MZ) twins, who share essentially all their DNA, and dizygotic (DZ) twins, who share about half. A larger MZ-versus-DZ gap signals a genetic contribution. Christiansen found pairwise concordance rates for criminal behavior of 35% for MZ males versus 13% for DZ males, and 21% for MZ females versus 8% for DZ females. The consistent doubling-or-more of concordance in identical twins points to a genuine genetic component in criminal liability, replicated in the same direction by Dalgard and Kringlen's Norwegian work.

Two features make this study more credible than the earlier twin research it built on. First, the sample is large and unselected, drawn from a complete regional twin register rather than from convenient or clinically referred cases, which had biased earlier studies toward inflated concordance. Second, the absolute concordance rates here are notably lower than in those earlier studies, which Christiansen took as a sign that better methodology yields more modest, more honest heritability estimates. He also noted higher concordance for serious and recidivist offenders than for petty ones, an early hint that persistent offending carries a stronger genetic loading than occasional crime.

The interpretive caveats are essential and Christiansen himself raised them. Concordance is far from 100% even in MZ twins, so environment clearly plays a large role; genes load the dice but do not determine the outcome. MZ twins also tend to share more similar environments and experience more mutual influence than DZ twins, which can inflate apparent genetic effects, the reason adoption studies like Mednick's are needed as a complement. As with all criminality genetics, what is inherited is not "crime" but correlated dispositions, and the male-female difference in concordance raises unresolved questions about sex-specific transmission.
