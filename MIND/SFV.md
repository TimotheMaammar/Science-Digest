# Short-form videos and cognitive/mental health

## About

## Synthesis

The most comprehensive meta-analysis on short-form video (SFV) use to date, synthesizing **71 studies** and **98,299 participants** across Asia, North America, and Europe. By focusing exclusively on **quantitative studies** of SFV platforms (e.g., TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts), this review isolates the effects of SFV from general social media use.

The central finding is that **higher SFV use is associated with lower cognitive performance** (average effect size *r = -0.34*), with the strongest negative correlations observed for **attention** (*r = -0.38*) and **inhibitory control** (*r = -0.41*). Similarly, SFV use is linked to **poorer mental health** (average effect size *r = -0.21*), with **stress** (*r = -0.34*) and **anxiety** (*r = -0.33*) showing the strongest associations. Notably, **no significant associations** were found with body image or self-esteem, suggesting that SFV content diversity may mitigate these effects.

Two moderator findings stand out. First, **addiction to SFV** (uncontrollable use) predicts cognitive and mental health outcomes more strongly than mere duration or frequency of use. Second, the negative correlations hold **across age groups** (youth vs. adults) and **platforms** (TikTok vs. general SFV), indicating robustness. However, the **cross-sectional nature** of 87% of the studies precludes causal inferences—it remains unclear whether SFV use *causes* cognitive decline or whether individuals with pre-existing attention deficits are more drawn to SFV.

The study also explores theoretical mechanisms, including **dual-process theory** (habituation to fast-paced content reducing endurance for slow tasks) and **neuroimaging evidence** (reduced P300 activity and structural differences in prefrontal cortex and reward circuits among heavy SFV users). Yet, these findings remain correlational, and the high heterogeneity (*I² > 95%*) suggests unmeasured moderators may play a role.
