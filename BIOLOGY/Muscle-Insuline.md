# Muscle mass and insulin resistance

## About

Title : Relative Muscle Mass Is Inversely Associated with Insulin Resistance and Prediabetes: Findings from the Third National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey

Links :

- <https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/96/9/2898/2834715>
- <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21778224/>

Researchers : Preethi Srikanthan, Arun S. Karlamangla

Date : 09/2011

## Synthesis

The foundational population study on the link between muscle mass and glucose metabolism, based on 13,644 participants from the nationally representative NHANES III survey. Skeletal muscle is the largest insulin-sensitive tissue in the body and is responsible for roughly 75 to 85% of insulin-stimulated glucose uptake, so the logic is direct: more relative muscle should mean better glucose disposal. The study confirmed this. Higher relative muscle mass, measured as skeletal muscle as a percentage of body weight, was inversely associated with insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) and with the prevalence of prediabetes and overt diabetes. Each 10% increase in the skeletal muscle mass ratio was associated with an 11% relative reduction in HOMA-IR insulin resistance and a 12% relative reduction in prediabetes prevalence, and these associations held after excluding people with diagnosed diabetes.

The mechanism and its causal direction are supported by prospective work that came later. A large cohort of young and middle-aged Korean adults found that relative muscle mass was inversely associated with the risk of developing type 2 diabetes in a dose-response manner across quartiles, and that two-year gains in muscle mass reduced diabetes incidence even in people with normal glucose tolerance. Because muscle contraction pulls glucose out of the blood partly through an insulin-independent pathway (contraction-activated GLUT4 transporters), muscle that is both abundant and used is what matters. This is why resistance training improves insulin sensitivity even before large changes in muscle size appear, and it fits the broader clinical picture where skeletal muscle insulin resistance is the earliest detectable defect in the road to type 2 diabetes, present decades before the pancreas fails.

It is worth noting, however, that what predicts insulin sensitivity is relative muscle mass, meaning muscle as a share of body weight, rather than absolute muscle mass. Fat is the factor that drags the picture down: a heavily muscled person who also carries substantial visceral fat can remain insulin resistant, because visceral fat drives inflammation that works against the protective effect of muscle. Muscle quality matters as well, since fat infiltration into muscle (myosteatosis) degrades its metabolic function regardless of size. This also means the standard HOMA-IR marker has a known limitation: some large studies have found that it can misclassify muscular people, and the raw association between fat-free mass and insulin resistance can look weak or even reversed once fat mass is accounted for. The robust and repeatedly replicated finding concerns relative muscle mass in a context of low fat, which is exactly the profile built by combining resistance training with cardiovascular activity and keeping body fat in check.
