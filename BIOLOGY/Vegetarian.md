# Vegetarian diets linked to higher mortality in older adults, in two very different countries

## About

Title : Vegetarian diets, dietary trajectories, and mortality risk among older adults: evidence from two nationally representative cohorts

Link : <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.numecd.2026.104963>

Researchers : Binyu Liu, Zhuoshuai Liang, Xiaoyang Li, Huizhen Jin, Ruofei Li, Hongrui Zhang, Jiahe Wang, Ran Tan, Yingao Xi, Yawen Jiang, Wenjing Sui, Yuan Zhang, Yong Li, Yunkai Liu, Yi Cheng, Yawen Liu

Date : 08/2026

## Synthesis

Vegetarian diets are usually assumed to lower mortality risk. This study tests that assumption specifically in adults aged 65 and over, using two nationally representative cohorts from culturally and nutritionally distinct settings: the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS, n = 6,678) and the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, n = 6,305), with vegetarian status harmonized across both based on animal-source food consumption.

**All-cause mortality, vegetarians vs omnivores:**

| Cohort | n | Hazard ratio | 95% CI |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CLHLS (China) | 6,678 | 1.19 | 1.07-1.32 |
| NHANES (US) | 6,305 | 1.52 | 1.11-2.08 |

No vegetarian subtype examined showed a protective association in either cohort.

**Dietary trajectories (CLHLS longitudinal analysis):**

- Participants who stayed consistently vegetarian over time: elevated mortality risk
- Participants who transitioned from omnivorous to vegetarian diet: elevated mortality risk

The authors argue from this that a single dietary snapshot understates what matters: long-term trajectory carries independent information about mortality risk beyond current status alone.

The association held across two populations with different food cultures, which weighs against a country-specific explanation, though it does not establish that vegetarianism causes higher mortality. The authors' preferred explanation centers on nutritional adequacy: restricting animal-source foods in older adults may make it harder to meet protein and micronutrient needs, particularly when replacement foods are not nutritionally equivalent. In the CLHLS, vegetarians were slightly older, more often female, more often urban, and differed in education and socioeconomic status from omnivores, factors that plausibly relate to both diet and mortality on their own.

Caveats: the design is observational (multivariable Cox proportional hazards models with time-varying covariates), so residual confounding cannot be ruled out despite adjustment. Reverse causation is also plausible: illness, frailty, poor appetite or socioeconomic hardship in older adults may drive a shift away from animal-source foods, making vegetarian status partly a marker of declining health rather than its cause.

The implication the authors draw is age-specific rather than general: the finding should not be read as vegetarian diets being intrinsically unhealthy, or as a reason for older adults to eat more meat. In this population, the "vegetarian" label describes what is excluded, not what replaces it, and whether the diet supplies adequate energy, protein, micronutrients and dietary diversity may matter more than the label itself, especially where maintaining muscle mass and functional capacity is already a concern.
