# Dietary fiber and health

## About

Title : Carbohydrate quality and human health: a series of systematic reviews and meta-analyses

Link : https://www.thelancet.com/article/S0140-6736(18)31809-9/fulltext

Researchers : Andrew Reynolds, Jim Mann, John Cummings, Nicola Winter, Evelyn Mete, Lisa Te Morenga

Date : 01/2019

## Synthesis

Commissioned by the World Health Organization to set an evidence base for how much dietary fiber people should eat, this is the most comprehensive analysis on the topic to date. It pooled nearly 40 years of research: 185 prospective studies and 58 randomized controlled trials, totaling almost 135 million person-years. Compared to the lowest consumers, the highest fiber consumers showed a 15 to 30% reduction in all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, as well as lower incidence of coronary heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes, and colorectal cancer. Each additional 8g of daily fiber was associated with a 5 to 27% drop across these outcomes.

The central practical finding is the dose. Risk reduction was greatest at a daily intake of 25 to 29g of total fiber, which became the basis for WHO and national recommendations. Crucially, the dose-response curves showed no clear ceiling: intakes above 29g appeared to confer even greater protection against cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and colorectal and breast cancer. This matters because most people fall far short, with average intake around 15g per day in the US and only 9% of UK adults reaching 30g. The randomized trials reinforced the observational data, showing that higher fiber intake also lowers body weight, systolic blood pressure, and total cholesterol, which strengthens the case that the relationship is at least partly causal rather than mere confounding.

A useful secondary result concerns carbohydrate quality more broadly. Whole grains showed benefits closely tracking those of fiber, with each additional 15g per day cutting mortality and disease incidence by 2 to 19%. By contrast, low glycemic index and low glycemic load diets offered only limited and inconsistent protection, mostly for type 2 diabetes and stroke. The authors note that low-GI foods can also be high in added sugars and saturated fat, which may blur their health signal. The takeaway is that fiber content and whole grains are far more reliable markers of healthy carbohydrate intake than glycemic index, and that for most people the single most actionable step is simply eating more fiber.
