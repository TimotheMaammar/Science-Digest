# Magnesium and testosterone

## About

Title : Effects of magnesium supplementation on testosterone levels of athletes and sedentary subjects at rest and after exhaustion

Links :

- <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20352370/>
- <https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12011-010-8676-3>

Researchers : Vedat Cinar, Yahya Polat, Abdulkerim Kasim Baltaci, Rasim Mogulkoc

Date : 2011

## Synthesis

The most cited interventional study on magnesium and testosterone, a 4-week trial across three groups: sedentary men taking 10 mg/kg/day of magnesium, tae kwon do athletes taking the same dose, and athletes taking no supplement. Magnesium supplementation raised both free and total testosterone in sedentary and active men alike, but the effect was larger in those who exercised. Reported increases in free testosterone were roughly 24% in the athletes and 17% in the sedentary group, with the combination of magnesium plus training outperforming training alone. This is supported observationally by Maggio et al. (2011), who found in 399 men aged 65 and over that serum magnesium was independently and positively associated with both testosterone and IGF-1, suggesting magnesium acts as a modulator of the body's overall anabolic status.

Three mechanisms are proposed. The most specific is modulation of sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG): magnesium appears to reduce testosterone's binding affinity to SHBG, which frees up more biologically active testosterone, explaining why free T tends to move more than total T. Magnesium is also a cofactor in the enzymatic reactions of steroidogenesis, and it improves sleep quality, which matters because most testosterone synthesis occurs during sleep. As with zinc, the effect is plausibly largest in people who are deficient, and magnesium deficiency is genuinely common given that modern diets and heavy sweating (relevant for athletes) deplete it.

The honest reading is that magnesium is a modest, real supporter of testosterone rather than a dramatic booster. It reliably raises free testosterone by something like 20 to 30% in the trial data, but it does not transform total testosterone, and the strongest effects appear in deficient or physically active men rather than in well-supplied sedentary ones. The evidence base is also thinner than it looks: the headline Cinar trial is small, short, single-center, and the dose used (10 mg/kg/day, around 750 mg for a 75 kg man) is well above typical supplemental doses. For an active person who sweats a lot and may be magnesium-deficient, it is one of the better-justified mineral choices, with the bonus of genuine benefits for sleep, blood pressure, and inflammation independent of testosterone.
