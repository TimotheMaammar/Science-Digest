# Microbiome and well-being

## About

Title : Microbiome and well-being: a meta-analysis

Link : https://www.nature.com/articles/s41522-025-00829-0

Researchers : Marta Kowal, Piotr Sorokowski, Oana David, Ioana Alexandra Iuga, Simone Renwick, Agnieszka Sorokowska

Date : 10/2025

## Synthesis

The first meta-analysis to examine the microbiome's link to psychological well-being rather than to disease, where almost all prior research had concentrated. Pooling 8 studies and 2,526 participants, it found that both microbial diversity and taxonomic abundance were positively associated with well-being, with diversity emerging as the stronger predictor (zr = 0.215 versus zr = 0.010 to 0.089 for abundance). The diversity association was notably clean, showing no statistical heterogeneity across studies. At the phylum level, only Bacillota (formerly Firmicutes) showed a significant positive link to well-being, while no single genus stood out, suggesting that overall microbial richness matters more than any particular microbe.

The proposed mechanisms run through the gut-brain axis, a bidirectional communication system between the digestive tract and the central nervous system. The gut produces over 90% of the body's serotonin and much of its dopamine, modulates immune and inflammatory responses, and synthesizes vitamins such as B12, B6, and K. A diverse microbiome is thought to help maintain optimal neurotransmitter levels, whereas dysbiosis can disrupt them. The authors also note that greater microbial diversity has been linked to more adaptive personality traits like extraversion and sociability, which may feed back into well-being through richer social connection, and that a more varied microbiome may act as a buffer that confers resilience against external disturbances.

One caveat is worth keeping in mind: all 8 included studies are cross-sectional, so causality cannot be established, and the relationship is likely bidirectional, since poor well-being can degrade the microbiome through worse diet, sleep, and stress just as easily as the reverse. The authors frame their results as preliminary but promising, and given that the microbiome is readily modifiable through diet, exercise, and lifestyle, the findings open a genuine avenue for future intervention research.
