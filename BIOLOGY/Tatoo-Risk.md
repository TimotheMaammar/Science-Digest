# Tattoo ink and the immune system

## About

Title : Tattoo ink induces inflammation in the draining lymph node and alters the immune response to vaccination

Link : https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2510392122

Researchers : Capucetti et al.

Date : 12/2025

## Synthesis

The first study to characterize what tattoo ink actually does to the immune response, rather than simply confirming that ink reaches the body's tissues. Using a mouse model with three inks (black, red, green) and validation on human cells in vitro, the researchers tracked the ink's journey through the lymphatic system. The ink drains into the lymph nodes within minutes of tattooing and is captured mainly by macrophages, specifically medullary macrophages. Crucially, this capture kills the cells through apoptosis, a result confirmed in both mouse and human macrophages, and triggers a pronounced inflammatory response that persists in the draining lymph node for at least two months after tattooing. The ink does not sit inertly in the skin: it relocates, accumulates, and provokes a sustained reaction.

The most novel finding concerns vaccination. When a vaccine was administered in the same lymphatic drainage area as the tattoo, the ink-laden lymph node altered the immune response, and not in a single direction. An mRNA COVID-19 vaccine (BNT162b2) produced a reduced antibody response, linked to lower expression of the Spike protein in the ink-burdened macrophages, suggesting that dying, ink-filled cells produce less of the vaccine antigen. By contrast, a UV-inactivated influenza vaccine produced an enhanced response. The takeaway is not that tattoos uniformly suppress immunity, but that they disrupt the normal functioning of the draining lymph node in ways that depend on the specific immune challenge.

Two caveats matter for interpreting this. First, the work is overwhelmingly based on mice; the only human component is the in vitro demonstration that ink kills human macrophages, and the vaccine effects have not been shown in people. Second, the vaccination effect depends on the shot being given in the same drainage territory as the tattoo, which is not always the case in practice. This is a mechanistic study that establishes a plausible and concerning pathway, not an epidemiological one that quantifies real-world risk. It pairs naturally with the observational work linking tattoos to lymphoma, which finds correlations but cannot prove causation.
